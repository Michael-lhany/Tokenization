Tokenization basics

What tokenization does

Language models do not consume raw text directly. They first break it into tokens, which can be whole words, subwords, punctuation marks, byte chunks, or single characters. The exact tokenizer is model-specific.

Section 1 Why it matters Click to hide or show

Tokenization affects cost, speed, multilingual support, and how well a model handles unfamiliar names or rare words. Smaller tokens give more flexibility, while larger tokens can be more efficient.

Common strategies

BPE, word-level, and character-level tokenization each make different tradeoffs between compactness and flexibility.

Section 2 Main tokenization techniques Click to hide or show

Most modern LLMs use subword tokenization, because it handles rare words better than pure word-level tokenization and keeps sequences shorter than character-level tokenization.

Byte Pair Encoding

Starts with characters and repeatedly merges frequent pairs into larger pieces. This is common in GPT-style systems and many LLaMA-family models, usually with a byte-level fallback so any text can be represented.

Examples: GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4, LLaMA, Mistral-style tokenizers.

WordPiece

Builds a vocabulary of subwords using a likelihood-based objective. It is widely used in BERT and related encoder models, and in some decoder models in the broader Transformer family.

Examples: BERT, ALBERT, many fine-tuned encoder stacks.

SentencePiece

Treats text as raw Unicode and learns subword units directly from data. SentencePiece can train BPE or Unigram tokenizers, and it is common in many multilingual models.

Examples: T5, FLAN-T5, XLNet, ALBERT, several multilingual LLMs.

Unigram

Starts with a large candidate vocabulary and removes pieces that are not useful. This often produces compact subword vocabularies and works well for multilingual text.

Examples: some SentencePiece-based models and multilingual systems.

Byte-level tokenization

Represents text at the byte level, which guarantees every input can be encoded. It is more robust to unusual symbols, but the token sequences can be longer.

Examples: ByT5, CANINE, and GPT-style tokenizers with byte fallback.

Character-level

Uses one token per character. This keeps the vocabulary tiny but produces very long sequences, so it is uncommon in large general-purpose LLMs.

Examples: some older RNN systems and byte/character research models.

Section 3 How major model families usually tokenize Click to hide or show

There is no single tokenizer for every model family, and even within the same family the tokenizer can change between versions. These are the common patterns you will usually see.

GPT-style models

Typically use BPE variants with byte fallback. This keeps the vocabulary compact and helps the model handle punctuation, code, and unusual words reliably.

LLaMA-style models

Usually use a SentencePiece-derived BPE tokenizer, again with byte-aware handling in many implementations. The exact vocabulary depends on the checkpoint.

BERT-style models

Usually use WordPiece. This works well for masked language modeling and encoder tasks, where the model benefits from stable subword boundaries.

T5 / FLAN-T5

Use SentencePiece, commonly trained with a unigram or subword objective. This gives a strong balance between vocabulary size and coverage.

ByT5 / CANINE

Use byte-level or character-aware tokenization. These models trade longer sequences for stronger robustness on noisy text and multilingual inputs.

Multilingual open models

Many multilingual LLMs use SentencePiece or byte-level BPE because they handle many writing systems and scripts more gracefully than word-only methods.
